# Obstruction Ledger F1.2 — Can an Off-the-Shelf Tool Detect Prose↔Table Numeric Inconsistency at TPR ≥ 0.90 / FPR ≤ 0.05?

## TL;DR
- **No off-the-shelf tool clears your pre-registered bar (TPR ≥ 0.90 AND FPR ≤ 0.05 on unseen documents).** The task you defined — automatically reconciling narrative numbers against the specific table cell they refer to, across arbitrary technical documents — is an open research problem. The single closest realistic data point in the literature (FinVerBench's best-calibrated LLM) reaches 79.0% recall at 0% false-positive rate on a narrow financial subset, which misses your recall floor; the only system reaching ~100% recall at ~0% FPR does so on an admittedly unrealistic "unrounded diagnostic" subset that the authors themselves refuse to treat as a leaderboard result.
- **The one true precedent that hits your bar (statcheck, 96.2–99.9% accuracy) works only because APA style rigidly mandates the format `t(28) = 2.20, p = .03`, which co-locates the linked numbers in one string.** Your domain has no such convention — which is exactly why your row-linked C1 found lexical label matches in only 6 sentences across 2 of 113 documents. statcheck confirms the linking problem is the whole game, and that it is solvable only where reporting conventions solve it for you.
- **The pragmatic move is to stop building the general interpretive check and narrow the product claim.** Either (a) restrict C1 to a regular sub-format you control (recompute-from-formula checks, GRIM-style arithmetic identities, or documents you emit yourself with known prose↔cell links), or (b) reposition C1 as a high-recall *triage* aid with a human in the loop, explicitly NOT a low-FPR autonomous checker. General-purpose fact-checkers (MiniCheck, AlignScore) do not handle tables or the linking problem and are not calibrated for numeric equality.

## Key Findings

**1. Grounded fact-verification / entailment tools (MiniCheck, AlignScore, SummaC, FactCC, QAFactEval, TRUE).** These are the most-cited, most-installable candidates, and none is fit for your task.
- **MiniCheck** (Tang, Laban & Durrett, EMNLP 2024, arXiv:2404.10774): the best small model, MiniCheck-FT5 (770M), reaches ~73.3% average balanced accuracy on the LLM-AggreFact benchmark (widely quoted as 74.7% in the FT5 blog write-up) versus GPT-4's 75.3%, "for 400x lower cost"; the newer Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B tops the leaderboard at ~77.4% balanced accuracy. It is pip-installable, on HuggingFace and Ollama, and runs >500 docs/min on a single A6000. **But**: (i) it operates over prose "grounding documents," not tables — LLM-AggreFact's 11 datasets are Wikipedia/news/dialogue/QA text, with no numeric-consistency-over-tables subset; (ii) it requires the (document, claim) pair as input — it does NOT solve the linking problem of which claim maps to which evidence; (iii) ~73–77% balanced accuracy is nowhere near your 0.90/0.05 bar even for prose. GitHub: Liyan06/MiniCheck, 219 stars, dormant since ~late 2024.
- **AlignScore** (Zha et al., ACL 2023): a 355M unified alignment metric, SOTA on SummaC/TRUE at release. Same limitations: prose-to-prose, no table encoder, no linking, and it returns a continuous alignment score rather than a numeric-equality judgment. GitHub: yuh-zha/AlignScore, 168 stars, low activity, no releases.
- **SummaC, FactCC, QAFactEval** are older NLI/QA-based summarization consistency metrics; on AGGREFACT-FTSOTA splits they score in the low-to-mid 60s balanced accuracy — below AlignScore and MiniCheck, and again prose-only.
- **Critically, no published evaluation of any of these covers numeric prose↔table consistency specifically.** They are designed to detect semantic unsupportedness in summaries and are known to be weak precisely on numeric/quantitative errors.

**2. Table-text entailment / table fact verification (TabFact, InfoTabS, SEM-TAB-FACTS, FEVEROUS, SciTab, TAPAS/TAPEX).** This area handles table evidence but is packaged as research checkpoints, not usable consistency-checking tools, and its accuracy is far below your bar in the numeric setting.
- **TabFact** (Chen et al., ICLR 2020; 16,573 Wikipedia tables, 117,854 human-annotated ENTAILED-or-REFUTED statements): the reference table-entailment dataset. SOTA on the official small test set has risen to 93.0% (PASTA + DATER, LLM-augmented) versus 92.1% human; strong fine-tuned models like PASTA alone reach 90.8%. **But** TabFact is binary entailment over clean two-label Wikipedia tables, with the (claim, table) pair given — no linking, no numeric-equality focus, and its claims are crowd-generated, not real prose.
- **FEVEROUS** (includes tables): TabVer (TACL 2024) is SOTA-competitive; on the numerical-reasoning subset even the best systems trail, and few-shot classification models "perform poorly."
- **SciTab** (Lu et al., EMNLP 2023, arXiv:2305.13186) is the closest to your domain — 1,225 real scientific-paper claims against real scientific tables, 3-class (supported/refuted/NEI). **GPT-4 reaches only 64.80 macro-F1 in the 3-class setting** (78.22 in 2-class); "all models except GPT-4 achieved performance barely above random guessing" (best open-source 3-class F1 ≈ 38). Human is ~84.7 F1 (3-class). The dominant error class is grounding errors (~50%) — the linking problem again — plus ~20% calculation errors.
- Nothing here ships as a drop-in tool; these are checkpoints/leaderboards. None reports FPR at the ≤0.05 level for numeric consistency on unseen documents.

**3. Numeric/quantitative consistency checking specifically.** This narrower area splits into (a) rigid-format recomputation tools that DO hit your bar in their niche, and (b) general numeric-hallucination detection that does not.
- **statcheck** (see Section 4) — the gold standard, but only because of APA format rigidity.
- **GRIM / GRIMMER / SPRITE** (Brown & Heathers, 2016–2017): arithmetic-possibility checks on reported means/SDs of integer data. In the original GRIM study of 260 leading-journal psychology articles, of the N=71 testable articles, 36 (50.7%) contained at least one impossible mean and 16 (>20%) contained multiple. These are essentially exact mathematical tests — very low FPR when applicable — but only apply to a narrow statistic (means of bounded integer data) and famously misfire when the granularity assumption is violated (means of multi-item scales step in 1/(3n), so a naive check flags legitimate means). Precedent value: like statcheck, they work by exploiting a mathematical constraint, not by interpreting prose.
- **XBRL / iXBRL validation** (SEC EDGAR, ESEF): validation rules check that tagged facts satisfy calculation relationships (e.g., assets = current + non-current) and data-type/unit constraints. Crucially, XBRL.org states plainly that "validation rules do not check the factual accuracy of data, simply whether it meets specific conditions." Consistency between a narrative number and a statement is achieved by *tagging both occurrences to the same concept* — i.e., the linking is done manually by a human tagger, not inferred. This is the industrial answer to your problem and it solves linking by fiat (human tagging), not by algorithm.
- **Number-hallucination in summarization**: benchmarks (FaithBench, FRANK, QAGS) show detectors achieve only ~50% accuracy on FaithBench; numeric errors are a known weak spot. No tool reports ≤0.05 FPR here.

**4. statcheck as the closest precedent — and why it does not transfer.**
- **Reported accuracy**: Nuijten et al. (2017) validity study found "statcheck's sensitivity (true positive rate) and specificity (true negative rate) were high: between 85.3% and 100%, and between 96.0% and 100%, respectively... The overall accuracy of statcheck ranged from 96.2% to 99.9%." An independent cyber-security replication (arXiv:2004.06672) reported a confusion matrix of accuracy .98, sensitivity 1.00, specificity .98, PPV .85, F1 .92. **This is the one precedent that clearly clears TPR ≥ 0.90 and FPR ≤ 0.05 — under the right conditions.**
- **The debate**: Schmidt (2016/2017, arXiv:1610.01010) argued statcheck cannot handle corrected p-values (Bonferroni, Greenhouse-Geisser), so legitimately corrected results get flagged as inconsistent (false positives) while uncorrected ones pass. His re-analysis concluded "Statcheck has poor sensitivity (.52) and poor validity (phi = .54): In 1,120 tests, the program scored 29 hits, committed 19 false alarms, and missed an estimated 27 truly inconsistent tests, while 435 tests went unrecognized. If Statcheck flags a test, it is correct in only 60.4% of all tests. If a test is truly inconsistent, Statcheck flags it in only 51.8% of cases." Nuijten et al. rebutted that such corrections are rare in practice. Both agree statcheck "occasionally misreads or misinterprets statistics."
- **How it solves linking**: it doesn't infer linking — APA style *co-locates* the linked numbers in a single regex-matchable string: `t(28) = 2.20, p = .03`. statcheck recognizes t, F, r, z, χ², and Q tests reported completely and in APA style, recomputes p from the test statistic and df, and compares. It detects only ~60% of reported NHST results — precisely the ones in the rigid format; the rest are invisible to it.
- **Implication for you**: statcheck proves mechanical numeric-consistency checking hits your bar ONLY where a mandated convention binds the numbers together lexically and specifies the mathematical relationship. Your domain has neither — which your own C1(b) measurement proved (verbatim label matches in 6 sentences / 2 documents / 113; zero co-located with numbers). The premise fails in exactly the way statcheck's premise holds. Maintenance: statcheck is the healthiest of the relevant repos — CRAN package, GitHub MicheleNuijten/statcheck ~187 stars, latest release v1.6.0 on 26 Jul 2024, plus a Python port and a Word add-in.

**5. Document-level numeric extraction and linking (FinQA, TAT-QA, MultiHiertt, LayoutLM/Donut).** These prove the linking capability exists inside QA models but is not exposed as a consistency checker.
- **FinQA** (Chen et al., EMNLP 2021, arXiv:2109.00122): 8,281 expert QA pairs over 2,789 report pages of S&P 500 filings requiring multi-step arithmetic; FinQANet (RoBERTa-large) scored ~61.2% execution accuracy vs. expert mean 91.16%, collapsing to ~22.8% on ≥3-step programs.
- **TAT-QA** (ACL 2021): hybrid table+text; TAGOP 58.0% F1 at release vs. 90.8% human; by late 2024 TAT-LLM (70B) reached ~88.4% F1. Text-only or table-only models fail immediately (BERT-RC 18.7% F1), confirming the task genuinely requires joint table+text alignment.
- **MultiHiertt** (ACL 2022): multiple hierarchical tables per doc; harder still.
- These systems perform alignment (which cell + which text span answer a question) *internally*, but they answer questions; none exposes an alignment/consistency API, and their end-task accuracy (~88% best-case, far lower multi-step) would not support a 0.05-FPR equality check. Document-understanding models (LayoutLM, Donut, DocFormer) provide layout/OCR structure — useful glue for extracting table cells with their labels — but do no consistency checking themselves.

**6. LLM-as-checker for this exact task.** Recent 2025–2026 work directly measures LLMs detecting numeric inconsistency between prose and tables, and the results are decisively negative on FPR.
- **FinVerBench** (arXiv:2605.29586): built from SEC 10-K XBRL filings for 43 S&P 500 companies; asks LLMs whether financial statements are numerically consistent. Headline finding: under a guided-checklist prompt on the unrounded diagnostic subset, **9 of 14 LLM runs produce 95–100% false positives on clean statements** — i.e., they flag almost everything. FPR across models spanned 0–100%. The single best-calibrated model (Claude Sonnet 4) achieved 0% observed FPR on 43 clean statements with 100% recall on the unrounded diagnostic variant, but on the realistic rounded variant its recall dropped to 79.0% at 0% FPR. The authors explicitly frame this as "a construct-validity conclusion rather than a final leaderboard," concluding the task "is not merely arithmetic detection, but calibrated judgment under incomplete observability." This is the strongest evidence that naive LLM deployment fails your FPR bar catastrophically, and even the best case misses your recall bar on realistic inputs.
- **Kensho FIND** (Lovering et al., ACL 2026 Findings, "On Finding Inconsistencies in Documents"): 500 long real documents (median ~60 pages) with expert-inserted inconsistencies spanning numeric, non-numeric, table, and text — the closest public benchmark to your exact task. Best model GPT-5 found 64% of inserted inconsistencies; a post-paper GPT-5.5 reached 76%; Gemini 2.5 Pro 61%. These are *recall/recovery* rates only, well below 0.90, and the benchmark does not report a low-FPR operating point at high recall. Experts noted reviewing just the tables in a company's annual report takes ~4 hours — the motivation for automation, but the models still miss a third to a half of issues.
- **Document inconsistency detection** (arXiv:2601.02627, ContraDoc): GPT-4o best recall ~0.878 with precision ~0.58 and FPR ~0.30 — nowhere near 0.05 FPR.
- **Bottom line**: across every 2025–2026 evaluation, no system reports simultaneous TPR ≥ 0.90 and FPR ≤ 0.05 on unseen documents for numeric prose↔table consistency. The best realistic simultaneously-reported point is FinVerBench's 79.0% recall @ 0% FPR (financial, narrow).

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Accept the negative result and stop building the general interpretive C1.** The evidence is consistent and strong: (a) statcheck/GRIM prove mechanical numeric checking hits your bar only where a rigid convention binds numbers together and defines the relation; (b) your own C1(b) measurement proves your domain has no such convention (6 label matches / 2 docs / 113, none co-located with numbers); (c) every 2025–2026 learned system (FinVerBench, FIND, ContraDoc) fails your 0.90/0.05 bar on unseen documents. There is no off-the-shelf tool, and no bounded glue-code assembly, that clears the bar for the general case. Treat "general prose↔table numeric consistency detection at 0.90/0.05" as an open research problem, not a product feature.

**Stage 1 — If you keep any numeric check, narrow it to a domain where the constraint is mechanical.** Three tractable narrowings, in order of ROI:
1. **Self-emitted documents**: if your system generates the reports, retain the prose↔cell provenance link at generation time (as writing-time approaches like EvidFuse/StructText do) and check equality against the known source cell. This converts an inference problem into a bookkeeping problem — you can plausibly hit your bar because linking is free.
2. **Recompute-from-formula / arithmetic-identity checks** (the statcheck/GRIM pattern): where a number is a deterministic function of others (totals, percentages, ratios, means of integer data), verify the identity. Very low FPR by construction; scope your product claim to "checks arithmetic identities," not "checks all numeric consistency."
3. **Regular sub-formats you can pattern-match** only where your corpus actually exhibits a stable convention — measure the base rate first (you already did; it was near zero, which is the answer).

**Stage 2 — If the use case is auditing arbitrary third-party documents, reposition C1 as human-in-the-loop triage, not an autonomous low-FPR checker.** Use a strong LLM (GPT-5.5-class) as a high-recall surfacer, explicitly accepting high FPR, and route candidates to a human. Benchmark against FIND (recovery rate) rather than promising 0.05 FPR. Set the product claim to "surfaces ~60–76% of inserted inconsistencies for human review," which is honest and matches the SOTA.

**Benchmarks/thresholds that would change this recommendation.** Revisit if any of: (a) a published system reports ≥0.90 recall AND ≤0.05 FPR simultaneously on a held-out numeric prose↔table benchmark (currently none does; watch the FinVerBench and FIND leaderboards); (b) a table-aware MiniCheck variant with a numeric-equality head ships with reported low FPR on real documents; (c) frontier LLMs on FIND exceed ~90% recovery with a calibrated low-FPR mode. Absent these, the honest answer stands: nothing off-the-shelf works, and the value is in narrowing the claim.

## Caveats
- **GitHub star counts are exact as of 13 Sept 2026** (MiniCheck 219, AlignScore 168, statcheck 187, Table-Fact-Checking 416), but last-commit dates could not be confirmed for the non-statcheck repos because GitHub renders them via JavaScript; maintenance status for those three is inferred from README/release signals (all appear dormant since ~2024). statcheck's July 2024 release date is firm.
- **statcheck's 96–99% accuracy is contested.** Schmidt's re-analysis argued real-world sensitivity and precision are considerably lower (~.52 sensitivity, ~60.4% precision when corrected p-values are present). The high figures assume APA-conformant, uncorrected results. Take the high numbers as an upper bound under ideal conditions.
- **FinVerBench's headline "100% recall @ 0% FPR" is on an unrealistic unrounded diagnostic subset of only 105 instances (43 clean, 62 injected) and one model (Claude Sonnet 4); the authors disavow it as a leaderboard result.** The realistic figure is 79% recall @ 0% FPR. Both are financial-statement-specific and may not transfer to your technical/research documents.
- **Benchmark tasks differ from yours in subtle but important ways.** TabFact/SciTab give the (claim, table) pair (no linking); FIND inserts inconsistencies (may not match your natural error distribution); FinVerBench checks statement-internal arithmetic, not free prose against a table. None is a perfect proxy, so treat cross-benchmark comparisons as directional.
- **"LLM-as-checker" costs and FPR are prompt- and version-sensitive.** FinVerBench shows the same model can swing from 0% to ~100% FPR based on prompt and rendering (rounded vs. unrounded), so any pilot must measure FPR on YOUR corpus, not trust published numbers.
- Some cited 2026 arXiv IDs (FinVerBench 2605.29586, Kensho FIND, ContraDoc 2601.02627) are very recent preprints; treat their exact numbers as provisional pending peer review.
